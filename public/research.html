The most striking finding from our 2026 commission survey isn't the average — it's the spread. Sellers who compared three or more agent proposals before committing clustered heavily in the 3.5–4.5% band. Sellers who signed with the first agent they met paid 5% or more at nearly three times the rate of comparison shoppers.

This gap isn't driven by market conditions or property type alone. When we controlled for price tier and metro area, the pattern held: sellers who structured a competitive process consistently extracted better pricing from agents. The most plausible explanation is negotiation leverage — an agent who knows they're competing against two or three alternatives prices their proposal differently than one who believes they're the only option on the table.

It's worth noting that commission is only one variable. Several high-commission agents in our sample delivered measurably better marketing outcomes: more photos, more days of active promotion, higher offer counts. But in the majority of cases — particularly in mid-range markets — the sellers who paid more did not report meaningfully different outcomes. The 1.5 percentage point average gap between traditional and competitive-proposal sellers, on a $400,000 home, represents $6,000. For most families, that is not a rounding error.

"When sellers receive multiple proposals, they stop accepting the first number an agent names."

Personal recommendations dominate agent selection — 61% of sellers named a referral from a friend or family member as a top-three factor. This shapes agent behavior as much as seller outcomes: realtors who invest heavily in past-client relationships and word-of-mouth networks consistently outperform cold-lead strategies. But for the seller, referral-based selection has a structural weakness — it limits the comparison set to agents already in the seller's social circle.

Commission rate ranked third, behind local knowledge but ahead of reviews. This ordering matters: it suggests sellers do care about cost, but only after they've satisfied themselves that the agent knows the market. An agent who can speak fluently about recent comps, local pricing trends, and neighborhood dynamics clears the trust threshold — after which commission becomes the key differentiator. The implication for agents is that leading with price competes on the wrong axis. Leading with local knowledge, then presenting a competitive rate, is the stronger approach.

Implication for sellers: Recommendation bias means most sellers never compare more than 1–2 agents — leaving commission savings and marketing quality on the table. Structuring a short competitive process costs nothing and takes less than a week.

The property-type spread in commission rates reflects two dynamics: transaction complexity and absolute dollar value. Condos average slightly higher than single-family homes (4.4% vs. 4.1%), likely because condo sales involve additional coordination — HOA documentation, association approval processes, and a buyer pool that's more often contingent on financing approvals. The additional administrative friction justifies a modest premium, and agents price for it.

Luxury properties tell the opposite story. At a 3.2% average, luxury sellers pay the lowest commission rate by percentage — but the highest in absolute terms. A 3.2% commission on a $1.5 million property is $48,000; the same percentage on a $350,000 single-family home would generate $11,200. Agents competing for luxury listings accept a lower percentage because the absolute payout is substantially larger, and sellers in the luxury segment are more likely to have the leverage and sophistication to negotiate. Multi-family investors exhibit similar dynamics — they transact more frequently, understand the economics precisely, and are accordingly more price-aware in agent negotiations.

The Agent Search Problem: Time & Cost Analysis

Published May 2026  ·  7 min read

74%

1.5%
average lower commission paid by sellers who compared 3+ agents
$6,000

The agent search problem is structural, not behavioral. Sellers don't contact only 1.3 agents because they're incurious — they do it because the dominant agent-selection channel (personal referrals) delivers a name, not a marketplace. When a trusted friend recommends an agent, evaluating additional options feels like doubting the recommendation. The result is a process that looks like a choice but functions more like a default.

The financial cost is measurable. Sellers who contacted three or more agents before selecting one paid on average 1.5 percentage points less in total commission than sellers who contacted one agent and signed. On a $400,000 home, that differential is $6,000. The process to obtain three competing proposals — posting a listing, receiving proposals, reviewing them — takes less time than most sellers spend researching which refrigerator to buy. The gap between the effort required and the financial outcome available is one of the more striking inefficiencies in residential real estate.
